When the world looks unfamiliar.
What should a detector do when it encounters something outside its training categories? I led a research group exploring unknown-object detection for autonomous driving.

Reproduce. Adapt. Inspect.
We reproduced Spatial Temporal Unknown Distillation, or STUD, using Python and Detectron2. My work combined research planning, environment setup, data preparation, and inspection of detection results.
Source: Project midterm record / STUD method credited to its authors

Different sources. One workflow.
The data work covered BDD one hundred K, COCO twenty seventeen, and YouTube VIS. Adapting formats and selected categories helped connect these sources to the reproduced baseline.

Inspect successes and false positives.
Further paper examples show unknown elephants and unfamiliar indoor objects. The elephant scene also contains a false pedestrian detection. These figures illustrate what to inspect; they are not newly generated outputs from our reproduced system.

AUROC measures separation.
The STUD paper reports an A U R O C of eighty-five point six seven percent on COCO, and seventy-five point six seven on nu Images. These are reference benchmarks for its BDD one hundred K trained detector. They are not final scores from my project.
Source: STUD, CVPR 2022, Table 1 / ResNet-50 / mean ± SD over 3 runs

Keep the evaluation convention explicit.
Higher A U R O C means better separation. Under the paper convention, in-distribution detections are positive. F P R at ninety-five is the share of unknown detections wrongly accepted as known when known detections reach ninety-five percent true positive rate.
Source: Metric interpretation follows the STUD paper / no reconstructed ROC curve

How much difficult weather?
The midterm plan proposed adverse-weather shares of twenty-five, fifty, seventy-five, and one hundred percent, keeping the training-set size fixed. These are proposed experiment settings, not measured improvements.
